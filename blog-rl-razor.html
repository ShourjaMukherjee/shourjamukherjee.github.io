Why RL fine-tuning forgets less than SFT

If you fine-tune a model on a new task with RL, it mostly keeps its scores on unrelated benchmarks. If you fine-tune it to the same accuracy with supervised fine-tuning, those scores drop. I had seen the plot from the RL's Razor paper a few times without understanding how they got from the plot to an explanation, so I went through it properly.

They start by looking for a single number that predicts how much a model forgets. To do that cheaply they use a toy task where an MNIST classifier has to say whether a digit is even or odd, and any even label counts as correct for an even digit, so there are many valid ways to be right. They fine-tune a lot of models with both methods and compare forgetting against candidates like how much the weights changed, how much the activations changed, and how far the model's outputs on the new task moved from the base model's outputs (measured with KL divergence). Output shift wins clearly. RL runs and SFT runs land on the same curve with an R² of 0.96 on the toy task and 0.71 in their LLM experiments, while the weight-change measures score between 0.34 and 0.58.

Then they check that the output shift is the real cause and that RL itself is incidental. In the toy task you can work out which labels keep the model as close as possible to its starting outputs while still being fully correct. SFT trained on those labels forgets even less than RL does.

That leaves the question of why RL shifts the outputs less. RL differs from SFT in two ways: it trains on the model's own samples, and it sees wrong answers and pushes away from them. They run all four combinations. The two methods that train on the model's own samples (GRPO, and a variant that only keeps the correct samples) both hold on to old skills. The two that train on outside data (plain SFT, and SimPO, which adds negative examples) both forget. So the part that matters is training on your own samples.

The theory section is easier to follow with numbers. Ask the base model "2 + 2?" and suppose it says "4" half the time, "four" 30% of the time and "5" 20% of the time.

"4""four""5"KL from base
Base model50%30%20%0
RL, keeping its own correct samples62.5%37.5%0%0.22
SFT on labels that all say "four"0%100%0%1.20

RL removes the wrong answer and leaves the 5:3 preference the model already had untouched. The SFT dataset knows nothing about that preference, so it drags the model all the way to "four", which is a much bigger move for the same accuracy. The paper proves that the "keep your own correct samples" distribution is the closest fully correct one to where you started, and that policy gradient with a 0/1 reward converges to it.

Two caveats from the authors. The proof is for a simplified setting, and they don't yet have an explanation for why a large shift on the new task hurts unrelated old tasks, so for now it is a measured relationship.

Paper: Shenfeld, Pari, Agrawal. RL's Razor: Why Online Reinforcement Learning Forgets Less (2025)
