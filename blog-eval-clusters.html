Eval questions that share a passage don't count separately

The standard error bar on an eval score assumes that every question is an independent sample, which is why more questions gives you a tighter bar. A lot of reading-comprehension style evals break that assumption, because they ask several questions about the same passage. If the model misreads the passage it tends to miss that whole group together, so those questions behave more like one sample than several.

Evan Miller's paper on eval statistics computes the standard error both ways on real evals, once treating the questions as independent and once grouping them by passage, which is called a clustered standard error.

DROP3.05×
MGSM1.88×
RACE-H1.10×

On DROP the clustered standard error is 1.34 against a naive 0.44, so a gap between two models that looked comfortable can sit inside the noise once you account for the grouping.

The same kind of correlation can work in your favour when you compare two models. Models tend to find the same questions hard, so you can take the per-question difference between the two and compute the error bar on that, which is tighter than comparing the two overall scores. The shared difficulty cancels out, and you get a narrower interval from exactly the same data.

In practice this comes down to two habits. If your questions share a passage, a document or a template, report clustered standard errors. If you are comparing two models on the same questions, use paired differences. For sizing, the worked example in the paper needs roughly 1,000 questions to reliably detect a 3-point difference.

Paper: Miller. Adding Error Bars to Evals: A Statistical Approach to Language Model Evaluations (2024)
